SECTION 1

The task and the two pipelines

A Local Climate Zone is a category describing the physical form of a piece of a city — how tall the buildings are, how tightly packed, how much vegetation. There are seventeen of them, ten built types and seven natural ones, and the goal is to produce a map of them from satellite data.

The input is never raw satellite imagery. It is a satellite embedding: a foundation model has already looked at a year of satellite observations for every point on the ground and compressed what it saw into a vector of numbers. Tessera version 2 produces 128 numbers per 10-metre pixel; Google's AlphaEarth produces 64. Those vectors are the input to everything described here. This matters enormously for interpreting the results, and it is the single most important fact on this page: the hard representational work was already done before any of these models saw the data.

Two different ways of turning those embeddings into a map were built, and they differ in what a single training example is.

Classification answers a coarse question and answers it well. Segmentation answers a much harder one — it has to place boundaries at 10-metre resolution — and it is scored, for comparability, by pooling its pixel predictions back up to the same 320 m squares the classifier works on. That pooling is what makes the two numbers on this page directly comparable.

Every architecture plugs into a shared registry. A family declares its name, which pipeline it serves, a ladder of five size presets, and a build function; the training loop, the evaluation code and the map-making code are all written against that interface and never branch on model type. Adding an architecture is one new file and one registration call.

Two of these ladders are not really ladders. The vision transformer's five presets collapse to three distinct sizes, because the modification that makes it work on small inputs overrides the very setting that distinguishes two pairs of presets. The dense-connection network's ladder runs out of order — its small preset is twice the size of its base — because the underlying published models are named by depth, not width. Both are worth knowing before treating "preset" as a capacity axis.

linear_probe

no spatial modelling · the measuring stick

Average the embedding across the whole 320 m square, standardise it, and fit a single layer of weights straight to the seventeen classes. That is the entire model: 2,193 numbers. It cannot see any structure inside the square — a high-rise block and an equal mix of park and tower would look identical to it.

Its purpose is not to win. It is the instrument that measures how much of the job the embedding has already done, and every other architecture on this page should be read as an answer to the question "how much better than this, and at what cost?"

Changed for this problem

Standardisation is a batch-normalisation layer with its learnable scale and shift switched off, rather than an external statistics file. This keeps the running mean and variance inside the saved model, so a checkpoint is self-contained and can make a map with nothing else alongside it. It has one size preset, deliberately: its parameter count is fixed by the embedding width and the class count, so the five-rung ladder it once carried was five identical models with different names.

mlp

no spatial modelling · added depth

The same averaging step, then a stack of fully-connected layers instead of one. The preset string spells out the hidden widths directly, so mlp_512-256 is two hidden layers. It tests whether the embedding needs a non-linear decision boundary or a straight one.

Changed for this problem

Pooling is masked: pixels with no valid satellite data are excluded from the average rather than counted as zero. Coastal and cloud-affected squares would otherwise have their means dragged toward a value no real surface produces. The model declares this capability to the training loop, which passes it a validity mask.

shallow_cnn

minimal spatial modelling

Two convolution blocks, each a 3×3 convolution followed by normalisation, a rectifier, and a halving of resolution; then average what remains and classify. On a 32-pixel square this leaves an 8×8 map before averaging, so the model can see that a square has tall structures on one side and open ground on the other.

This is the smallest architecture on the page that models spatial arrangement at all, and the gap between it and the linear probe is the cleanest available measurement of what spatial structure inside a 320 m square is worth.

Changed for this problem

Convolutions use reflected padding rather than zeros, so the edge of a square is continued rather than surrounded by a value that means nothing in embedding space. Resolution-halving rounds up, so the model stays valid if someone trains on smaller squares. It takes no validity mask: a convolution mixes valid and invalid pixels across its receptive field long before the averaging step, so a masked average here would be quietly misleading rather than helpful.

aspp

multi-scale context in one layer

Four convolutions run in parallel over the same input, each looking at a different spatial extent: one at a single pixel, one spread over a small neighbourhood, one spread wider, and one that averages the entire square. Their outputs are stacked, compressed back to a single width, averaged and classified.

The idea is that a climate zone is defined jointly by fine texture and by neighbourhood context, and a single parallel layer can capture both without the depth a conventional network needs. It reaches the same receptive breadth as a much deeper stack at a fraction of the cost.

Changed for this problem

This is a reduced version of a module normally used as a segmentation head, with the spatial extents tuned down to suit a 32-pixel square — a wide dilation calibrated for a 224-pixel photograph would reach past the edge of the input entirely. The global-average branch is resized back to full size before stacking.

mobilenet · resnet · efficientnet · densenet · convnext

borrowed image-recognition backbones

Five families of established image classifiers, each offering a five-rung ladder from roughly 600,000 to 196 million parameters. They differ in how they build depth — residual shortcuts, dense concatenation, depth-separable convolutions with squeeze-and-excite gating, or a modernised convolution stack — but they share an origin: all were designed to classify 224-pixel photographs into a thousand everyday categories.

That origin is the problem, and fixing it is the single most consequential modification in this codebase.

Changed for this problem

Every one of these networks reduces its input by a factor of 32 on the way through. Fed a 32-pixel square, they arrive at the classifier with a single pixel — all spatial structure destroyed before the first block finishes. An automatic repair walks the network in execution order and relaxes each resolution-reducing step until at least a 4×4 map survives: large-kernel stems become 3×3 with no stride, smaller convolutions keep their kernel but lose their stride, and pooling layers are removed. Any change that fails to increase the map is reverted, which is what correctly skips squeeze-and-excite branches, whose pooling is a side path. Weights are never loaded from photographs; a representation learned on natural images has no relationship to a satellite embedding channel.

vit

attention over patch tokens

A vision transformer cuts the input into square tokens, embeds each one, and lets every token attend to every other. With no built-in assumption that nearby pixels are related, it must learn spatial relationships from data — an approach that rewards very large training sets.

Changed for this problem

The token size is forced from 16 pixels down to 2. At the published setting a 32-pixel square yields four tokens, which is not enough for attention to do anything; at 2 pixels it yields 256. This is the transformer equivalent of the stem repair above — and it is also why two pairs of presets collapse into each other, since the published models they name differ only in the token size this override replaces.

SECTION 3

Segmentation architectures

Four families. All must return a prediction for every input pixel at exactly the input's size, which is a stricter contract than it sounds — batches are padded to the largest tile in the batch, so odd sizes genuinely occur and every family handles them explicitly.

unet

encoder–decoder with skip concatenation

The input is passed down through several stages, each a pair of convolutions followed by a halving of resolution, building up abstract features while losing spatial precision. A bottleneck sits at the bottom. The decoder then climbs back up, and at each step it concatenates the matching encoder stage alongside the upsampled features before convolving. Those skip connections are what restore the fine detail the downward path discarded.

Sizes are set by two numbers: how many stages deep, and how wide the first stage is, with width doubling each stage. The small preset is three stages starting at 32 channels; large is four starting at 48.

Changed for this problem

Reflected padding throughout, for the same reason as the shallow network — zero-padding a tile edge invents embedding values. Dropout is applied only at the bottleneck, where features are most abstract and most prone to memorising particular cities. When an upsampled feature map and its skip disagree in size by a pixel, the decoder resizes to the skip rather than cropping, which is what makes odd-sized padded batches safe.

fcn8

score fusion · the parameter-efficiency champion

The older idea, and structurally the more interesting one. The same convolutional encoder runs down through two or three stages. Then, rather than carrying rich features back up, each stage is collapsed immediately to seventeen numbers per position — one score per class — by a single 1×1 convolution. The coarsest score map is doubled in size and added to the next finer one, repeated down the pyramid, and the result resized to the input.

The difference from the U-shaped network is precise and worth stating plainly: that one concatenates features and lets the decoder decide what to do with them, while this adds class evidence and has already thrown away everything that was not evidence. That is why it is so much smaller — and, as the results show, why it loses surprisingly little by it.

Changed for this problem

A miniature of the published design. The original uses a sixteen-layer photographic backbone reducing by 32×; this uses two or three stages reducing by 4× or 8×, because a 128-pixel tile cannot survive the original's downsampling. Depth is capped at three by the presets — the design fuses exactly three scales, and the larger presets widen rather than deepen. The network's two dense layers are replaced by a single convolution block, which is the defining move of the fully-convolutional idea and is preserved here.

attention_unet

gated skips · a control, not a candidate

The U-shaped network with one addition: before each skip connection is concatenated, it passes through a gate. The gate takes the decoder's upsampled features as a signal, combines them with the skip, and produces a weight between zero and one for every pixel, which scales the skip down where it is judged irrelevant.

It subclasses the plain U-shaped network and adds nothing else, so the two differ only by the gates — a measured 1.0 to 1.1 per cent of parameters at every preset. Any difference in results is therefore attributable to the mechanism rather than to capacity.

Changed for this problem

Registered with an explicit prediction that it will not help, recorded in the code before it was run. Gates were designed for networks trained from raw intensity images, where shallow skips carry low-level edges and texture the decoder must learn to ignore; the gate is what arbitrates that gap. Here the encoder is fed foundation-model embeddings, so every skip — including the first — already carries semantic features, and there is no low-level tier for a gate to suppress. The honest prior is that it lands inside seed noise.

resnet_unet

residual encoder, U-shaped decoder

A U-shaped decoder mounted on a published residual image-recognition backbone, taking skip connections from its four stage outputs. It is by far the largest segmentation family, starting at 14 million parameters where the plain U-shaped network starts at 39 thousand.

Changed for this problem

The same input-size repair as the classification backbones, applied by hand rather than automatically: the 7×7 strided stem becomes 3×3 unstrided and the pooling layer is removed, taking the encoder's total reduction from 32× down to 8×. Note a real gap — this family defines only three of the five presets, so the command line's default of large raises an error rather than running.

SECTION 4

Modifications that matter

Five changes cut across every family. One of them was worth more than every architectural choice combined, and it was a bug fix.

Input size
Relaxing the downsampling stems

Until September 2026 only the residual family had this repair, applied by hand. Every other borrowed backbone was arriving at its classifier with a 1×1 feature map — being handed a single pixel and asked to name a climate zone. Those families clustered at agreement 0.599 to 0.601 regardless of whether they carried 604 thousand or 12.9 million parameters, which is exactly the signature of models that are all receiving the same degraded input. Generalising the repair lifted the small efficient network to 0.6615, the best honest classification result in the campaign. Every measurement from these families taken before this date is void.

Padding
Reflected, never zero

Zero is a meaningful value in an embedding channel, not a neutral one. Padding a tile edge with zeros invents a surface that does not exist; reflecting continues the real one.

Missing data
Masked pooling where it is honest, and only there

Families that pool directly — the probe and the fully-connected stack — declare that they accept a validity mask and exclude invalid pixels from their averages. Convolutional families deliberately do not, because a convolution has already mixed valid and invalid pixels across its receptive field before any pooling happens, so a masked average would carry false authority.

Odd sizes
Resize to the skip, never crop

Segmentation batches are padded to the largest square in the batch, so odd dimensions genuinely occur at city edges. Every decoder resizes to match its skip connection rather than cropping, and every family returns a map at exactly the input size — the loss, the metrics and the pooling back to 320 m squares all index masks of that shape.

No transfer
Never load photographic weights

Pre-trained weights are refused whenever the input is not three-channel colour, with a warning. A filter learned on natural images has no relationship to a channel of a learned satellite embedding, and loading one would be worse than random initialisation.

Augmentation is restricted to operations that move pixels exactly — horizontal and vertical flips, quarter-turn rotations, and additive noise. Nothing rotates by an arbitrary angle or rescales, because both would interpolate between embedding vectors, and the space between two embedding vectors is not guaranteed to mean anything. In segmentation the mask receives every geometric transform the image does and never receives noise.

One honest caveat about early stopping. Across the sixteen segmentation rows, training length ranged from 12 to 45 epochs with no predictable relationship to embedding or architecture. The worst-scoring row stopped at 12 epochs. Some of what reads below as an architectural difference may be a training-length difference, and with one seed per cell there is no way to separate them from these runs alone.

SECTION 6

How the data is split

This section decides whether any number on this page means anything. The dataset covers 51 cities. How you divide them determines whether you are measuring a model that understands urban form, or one that has memorised particular cities.

The underlying problem is spatial autocorrelation. Two 320 m squares a kilometre apart in the same city are not independent observations — they share a construction era, a planning code, a climate, and often a satellite pass. A model that has seen one has, in effect, half-seen the other. Split the data by cutting each city into a checkerboard and you will measure something that looks excellent and predicts nothing about a new city.

The three modes, and what each is worth

Quarantined

within-city grid

Each city is tiled into 3×3 blocks and the tiles are dealt into training, validation and test. Train and test end up interleaved across the same neighbourhoods.

Kept only for single-city demonstrations. Cannot carry a headline result.

Honest

global · "cultural"

Splits by city. Ten cities — Guangzhou, Jakarta, Moscow, Mumbai, Munich, Nairobi, San José, Santiago, Sydney, Tehran — are held out entirely. The model never sees a pixel of them during training.

Chosen to span climates and building cultures. This is the only mode that answers the question the project exists to ask.

Leaky

orig_test

A hybrid that keeps the dataset's original test patches but folds every city into training. All ten test cities are also training cities.

Measured inflation: +21 to +29 points of agreement. Useful as a cheap ranking signal, never as a reported value.

Why segmentation needs three extra rules

Splitting by city is straightforward when a training example is a 320 m square. It is not straightforward when a training example is a 1,280 m tile, because a tile is sixteen times the area and can straddle a boundary the patch-level split assumed was clean. Three rules close that gap, and all three are enforced in the split code rather than left to the person running it.

Rule 1
Tile purity — drop, never majority-assign

A tile whose patches carry more than one split assignment is discarded outright. This is the segmentation-specific leak with no analogue in the classification pipeline: a 1,280 m tile is a single training example, so one test patch inside it contaminates the whole thing. Majority-assigning would hide the contamination rather than remove it.

Rule 2
A proximity buffer of 1.3 km

A tile is dropped when it comes within 1.3 km of a patch belonging to a different split. The original design called for buffering a dividing meridian in each held-out city — but no such meridian exists; the validation and test patches interleave, and in Santiago the validation range sits entirely inside the test range. The buffer was therefore implemented as what it was actually for, without assuming a geometry the data does not have.

Rule 3
Held-out cities contribute no training tiles

Only one city is affected. Guangzhou carries both training and test patches, so "the training cities" and "the held-out cities" genuinely overlap by one. Its training patches sit about 24 km from its test patches and would probably survive the buffer — but a city appearing on both sides needs a recorded decision rather than an accident. All 249 of its training tiles are dropped, costing 1.6 per cent of the training pool and keeping the comparison against the published benchmark exact.

Rule 4
Six inner-validation cities, for early stopping

A subtler point. The held-out cities' validation and test patches are the same ten cities, a median 2.55 km apart — so anything selected on that validation set can read city-specific structure off the test set. Early stopping is a low-degrees-of-freedom fit, but it is not zero. Six cities are therefore held out of the training pool instead, and used for stopping: Cairo, Istanbul, London, Melbourne, Rio de Janeiro and Vancouver. Selection is deterministic and stratified by continent — six cities picked at random could easily take four from Europe, making the stopping signal a European one — and weighted toward the largest available city in each region, since an unweighted pick can return a city with a single tile.

Those proportions differ sharply and deserve comment. By city role, segmentation's tiles divide 35 training cities, 6 inner-validation, 10 held-out. But by tile count the test set is only 7 per cent, because the three purity rules fall almost entirely on the held-out cities — they are the only ones with mixed assignments to purge. In the best run, 1,252 tiles were dropped to the buffer, 376 to mixed assignment and 342 for having no labels at all.

That attrition creates a real measurement problem: scored strictly, the surviving tiles cover only 41.2 per cent of the available test patches, which is too narrow a base and a biased one — the tiles that survive purity filtering are the spatially uniform ones, which are also the easy ones. A second scoring pass fixes it. It re-runs the finished model across the held-out cities with purity and buffer filtering switched off, which is safe precisely because a forward-only pass has no leakage to protect against. Coverage rises to 83.8 per cent (20,245 of 24,170 patches), and those are the numbers reported below.

SECTION 8

Why some models win

Seven hypotheses, ordered by how much evidence stands behind them. H1 to H5 and H7 rest on measured numbers reproduced below each one; H6 is explicitly unresolved and says so.

H1

The embedding matters more than everything else combined

This is the strongest result on the page and the one that should shape the next six months of work. Holding the architecture fixed and swapping the embedding moves the number further than swapping across the entire architectural range holds it.

Why: the two embeddings encode different things. Prior analysis found AlphaEarth to be the most geographically clustered of the three embeddings tested — a classifier can separate its cities almost perfectly — while Tessera clusters less tightly. An embedding that encodes where you are more strongly than what is there gives a model an easy shortcut that does not survive the trip to an unseen city.

H2

Capacity is anti-correlated with accuracy, and the ceiling is low

A linear probe with 2,193 parameters and no ability to see spatial structure reaches 82 per cent of the best model's score. A two-block network with 23,697 parameters reaches 93 per cent of it. The curve peaks somewhere between 600 thousand and 1.6 million parameters — the exact location varies by embedding — and turns downward above it on every embedding tested.

Why: the foundation model was trained on vastly more data than the 342,000 labelled squares available here, and it has already solved the representation problem. What remains is a decision problem over an already-good 128-dimensional feature space, and a seventeen-way decision in 128 dimensions does not need 21 million parameters. What a large backbone does with its surplus capacity is memorise the idiosyncrasies of the 42 training cities, and those do not transfer.

H3

The gain comes in two steps, and the cheap step is the bigger one

Held to a single embedding, the classification ladder has three segments, not a smooth curve. Going from no spatial modelling to minimal spatial modelling is worth +7.0 and costs 21,504 extra parameters. Going from there to the peak is worth +4.8 and costs 1.53 million — about seventy times the parameters for two-thirds the gain. Going beyond the peak is worth −7.0, giving back the entire first step.

Why the first step is so cheap and so large: a 320 m square containing a tower beside a park is a different climate zone from one of uniform mid-rise, and an averaged embedding cannot tell them apart because averaging destroys exactly that distinction. An 8×8 feature map recovers it. Beyond that first discrimination there is far less additional structure inside 320 m than a network designed for 224-pixel photographs assumes is there.

Note the first step is much smaller on the weaker embedding — +2.5 rather than +7.0 — which suggests AlphaEarth's within-square spatial detail is itself less informative, not merely its pooled summary.

H4

Segmentation still has capacity headroom where classification does not

Segmentation is the one place capacity is still buying something. On Tessera both families gain strongly from nano to small with no sign of a plateau — the opposite of the classification picture.

Why: the two tasks are not equally hard. Classification labels a 320 m cell, a job the pooled embedding nearly solves on its own. Segmentation must place boundaries at 10 m. The encoder is still being handed a solved representation, but the decoder is learning geometry from scratch, and geometry is where the parameters go. This is the reasoning behind the larger segmentation run currently training.

Tessera fcn8  nano 0.4449 → small 0.5102  =  +6.5
H5

Concatenating features beats adding scores — but barely, and at 15× the cost

The U-shaped network wins on Tessera, by 1.1 points at small and 0.4 points at nano — the latter inside noise. For that it pays 15 times the parameters.

Why the margin is so small: the difference between the two designs is that one carries full encoder features up to the decoder, and the other collapses each scale to seventeen class scores first and discards everything else. Throwing away non-evidence is normally costly — but here the encoder's features are already semantic, because they started life as foundation-model embeddings. There is much less non-evidence to lose than the original design assumed. The smaller network is the clear efficiency winner and should be the default for anything resource-constrained.

H6

The weaker embedding inverts the capacity curve — tentative

On AlphaEarth the capacity ordering breaks down entirely. The U-shaped network gets worse from nano to small, while the score-fusion network gets better. On Tessera both improve.

A plausible reading is that a 64-dimensional, heavily geography-clustered embedding offers city identity as a faster-descending gradient than class identity, so added capacity finds the shortcut first. But the worst-scoring row stopped training at 12 epochs, and an under-trained model and an over-fitted one are not distinguishable from a single seed. Treat this as unresolved until it is re-run with more seeds.

H7

Leakage is not a constant offset, and it reorders the models

It is tempting to treat the leaky split as a cheap screening tool on the assumption that it shifts every model by the same amount. It does not. Across the seven completed pairs the rank correlation is 0.643, which at this sample size is not distinguishable from chance. Only the top two rows hold their position; one model moves three places up and another three places down.

More revealing is which models gain. The AlphaEarth rows gain an average of +27.1 points from leakage where the Tessera rows gain +23.4 — and the single largest beneficiary, moving from sixth to third, is an AlphaEarth model. That is precisely what H1 predicts: an embedding that encodes location strongly offers more to memorise, so it profits more when the test cities are in the training set. The leakage differential is itself evidence for the embedding hypothesis.

The practical conclusion is the restrictive one. A leaky run cannot screen candidates, cannot rank them, and cannot be reported. Its only remaining use is as a sanity check that a pipeline runs end to end.

SECTION 9

The way forward

The evidence points one direction: stop spending effort on network design, and spend it on supervision, evaluation and combination. Ordered by expected return per unit of effort.

Stop climbing the capacity ladder

For classification this question is answered. Nine seeds of the residual family, five architectural philosophies and four orders of magnitude all land in the same band. The two runs currently training are the last capacity questions worth asking: one tests whether the classification ceiling is real (predicted: no improvement over 0.6606), the other whether segmentation's climb continues (predicted: the only row with a genuine chance of beating 0.5210). After those, close the line.

In flight

Report per-city results, not a pooled number

The single largest gap in the current reporting. A per-city spread of 0.18 means the pooled figure describes no actual city. The best model is near-unusable somewhere and good somewhere else, and which is which is the scientifically interesting part. Every finished run already wrote per-patch probabilities with aligned city labels, so this costs no training time at all — it is a table waiting to be computed.

No GPU

Compute the missing region-overlap figure

Region overlap is disabled on the pooled scoring path, so segmentation runs report it only at pixel level and the leaky runs not at all. Every run wrote its confusion matrix to disk; the statistic is recoverable from those files without re-running anything, and it is the metric segmentation work is normally compared on.

No GPU

Attack the transfer failure directly, class by class

The clearest diagnostic in the campaign: in Nairobi, Open Low-Rise is 22.3 per cent of the ground truth. A model trained with Nairobi predicts about 21 per cent of it. The honest classification model predicts zero, and the honest segmentation model 3 per cent. The class is perfectly learnable and completely fails to transfer — which is a supervision problem and a domain-shift problem, not a capacity problem. No architecture in this registry will fix it.

Highest value

Combine models rather than enlarging them

Already demonstrated in this project and still the best lever available. A leave-one-city-out weighted ensemble reached 0.6871 against a best single model of 0.6497, and adding freely-available structural data — building heights, built-surface fraction, tree canopy — through a frozen correction pathway reached 0.7055 to 0.7081 with no training at all. Both exceed the entire architectural span by a wide margin.

Proven

Test output resolution before testing topology

The only change that has ever moved segmentation numbers in this project was a resolution fix, not an architectural one. The machinery to compare 10 m native output against 100 m smoothed output already exists and has been run for three cities; extending it is cheap and tests something the results say actually matters.

Cheap

Repair the three broken size ladders

Housekeeping with real consequences for interpretation. The residual U-shaped family defines three of five presets, so the command-line default raises an error. The transformer's five presets are three distinct models. The dense-connection family's ladder is out of order, with small twice the size of base. Until these are fixed, "preset" is not a capacity axis and any plot against it is misleading.

Correctness

Add seeds before adding architectures

Most cells in the matrix are a single seed against a measured noise band of ±0.0079. Several differences reported above are smaller than that. Three seeds on the six most informative cells would buy more interpretive certainty than any new family — and would settle whether H6 is a real inversion or an artefact of a run that stopped at 12 epochs.

Rigour

The one-sentence version. The foundation model already did the hard part; from here, accuracy comes from better labels, honest evaluation and combining predictions — not from a bigger network.
